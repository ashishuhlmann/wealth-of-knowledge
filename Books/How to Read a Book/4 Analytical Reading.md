Question: What is this author's understanding?

Observe the title.

Ask questions while reading that you yourself must try to answer in the course of reading.
1. What is the book about as a whole?
2. What is being said in detail and how?
	- What are the main arguments?
3. Is the book true, in whole or part?
4. Why is this significant?
	- Why does the author think it is important to know these things?

**conceptual note-making**
- answers to the truth and significance of the book
- notes made on the pages themselves

"To make knowledge practical we must convert it into rules of operation. We must pass from knowing *what is the case* to knowing *what to do about it if we wish to get somewhere*" (66)

**theoretical book** - books teaching that something is the case
- **philosophical theory** - theoretical work dealing with facts that can be checked in daily experience
- **scientific** - theoretical work dealing with facts that must be checked in specific environments

"Does something exist? What kind of thing is it? What caused it to exist, or under what conditions can it exist, or why does it exist? What purpose does it serve? What are the consequences of its existence? What are its characteristic properties, its typical traits? What are its relations to other things of a similar sort, or of a different sort? How does it behave?" (93-94)

**practical book** - books teaching how to do something

"What ends should be sought? What means should be chosen to a given end? What things must one do to gain a certain objective, and in what order? Under these conditions, what is the right thing to do, or the better rather than the worse? Under what conditions would it be better to do this rather than that?" (94)
## Stage I - Contents

1. Classify the book according to kind and subject matter.
	- Are you reading a novel, play, epic, lyric? Or a practical or theoretical book?
2. State the **unity** of the whole book is about in one sentence.
	- one or two sentences that describe the entire book
3. Enumerate its major parts in their order and relation, and outline them.
	- Do the premade chapters and sections make the best possible groupings?
	- This is only an approximation.
4. Define the problem or problems the author is trying to solve.
### Example

Unity of *Odyssey*

"A certain man is absent from home for many years; he is jealously watched by Poseidon, and left desolate. Meanwhile his home is in a wretched plight; suitors are wasting his substance and plotting against his son. At length, tempest-tossed, he himself arrives; he makes certain persons acquainted with him; he attacks the suitors with his own hand, and is himself preserved while he destroys them" (79)
## Stage II - Interpretation

**word** - one single unit of meaning
- can be used ambiguously, since words have multiple meanings
- every field of knowledge has specific technical vocabulary
- can be used in a single sense or a combination of sensese

**term** - basic element of communicable knowledge
- word or phrase used unamiguously
- skilled use of words for communicating knowledge

Each term is like a part of a jigsaw puzzle that is already in place, where the reader must fill in the meaning to the rest of the words used.
- This assumes that the book is ideal and all fits together, which is not always the case.

Question: Why instead, do authors not simply write entirely in terms?

5. Find the important words and come to terms with the author.
6. Graso the author's leading propositions by dealing with his most important sentences.
7. Know the arguments by finding them in or constructing them out of sequences of sentences.
8. Determine which of the initial problems the author has solved and which of them they did not. Check if the author knew what he did not solve.
## Stage III Criticism

Understand before judging. Then ask, is it true? What of it?
- Understanding is stating it in your own words.
- Failure to understand is also a critical judgement, but only after trying everything does it reflect on the book rather than on yourself. In rare cases, a book is unintelligible.

9. Having completed an outline and interpretation of the book, either agree, disagree, or suspend judgement.
	- Do not disagree disputatiously or contentiously.

Learning the truth matters more than winning the argument.
- "He should be as prepared to agree as to disagree" (147)

If no reason is given for a proposition, it is a expression of personal opinion.

Recognize the difference between knowledge and mere personal opinion by presenting good reasons for critical judgement.
- Show wherin the author is **uninformed**. They lack a piece of knowledge relevant to the problem.
- Show wherin the uathor is **misinformed**. They assert something that is not the case.
- Show wherin the author is **illogical**. They committed one of two reasoning fallacies.

**non sequitur** - the conclusion reached does not follow the line of reasons offered
**inconsistency** - two statements are incompatible with one another

If you fail these three criteria, you must either agree or suspend judgement, in light of one last criteria.
- Show wherein the author's analysis or account is incomplete. They have not solved all the problems they started with. Or, they have not made the best use of all their materials. Or, they did not see all their implications, and they failed to make important distinctions.

Agreement is not a choice. Liking or disliking the truth is a choice.

Reguard disagreement as capable of being resolved.
- Do not disagree hopelessly. All rational men can agree.
- Disagreement is not only a result of misunderstanding.

"Good controversy should not be a quarrel about assumptions" (155)
- If an author asks you to take something for granted, honor the request in order to see their case through.
