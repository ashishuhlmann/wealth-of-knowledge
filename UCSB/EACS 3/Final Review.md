## Chinese History

**Bronze Age**

The Bronze Age in China was after 2000 BCE, when weapons and vessels were made from bronze. The the Confucian ritual state lectured showed that this time was made up of royal clans with feudal lords across the kingdom. There was strong sacred kingship, sacrifcice, and ancestor worship. The mandate of heaven was also used to determine who should stay in power and who should leave. All kingdoms at this time were scattered which lead to the social distintegration in the Spring and Autumn Period, which would eventually lead to the Warring States period.

**Spring and Autumn Period**

The Spring and Autumn period was a time between the eighth and fifth centuries B.C. where social disitringration started as a result scattered feudalism and decentered kingdoms. The Confucian Ritual State lecture showed a gradual decrease in royal power with local groups gaining their own independent political power. Ritual was still a component in China with the bronze tiger and cauldron, representing offers to ancestors and military authorization; however, states began to annex each other and claim smaller ones which resulted in a need for ethics and leadership. This period is significant because it leads up to the Warring States period and brings the hundred schools of thought in attempts to solve China's problems.

**Warring States Period**

The Warring States period was a time in China between the fifth and third centuries B.C, where social disintegration lead to intense warfare between states. The Confucian Ritual State lecture showed the addition of iron technology in warfare. Nobility in feudal kingdoms stopped recognizing the kings and wanted to develope their own kingdoms as unrest spread across the population. It ended in the wars of conquest into the Qin dynasty. This period is significant for its many philosophers that tried to influence courts and contending states with their own ideas for models of governments, with Confucius and Laozi being among them in the hundred schools of thought. 

**hundred schools of thought**

The hundred schools of thought refers to the many philosphical ideas proposed around the Warring States period with many ideas on how governments should run. The Confucian ritual state lecture showed two of the ideas: rujia, which advocated for less military force and less punishment with more social ethics, education, and ritual; and legalism, which advocated for objective law and heavy social engineering. This time is significant for the collection of Confucian teachings on self cultivation as well as the *Daodejing* which talked about living by the Dao or the Way. Confucian ideas met China's problems of conflict with the middle path, kinship ethics, human relationship, and ideas of hierarchies. The hundred schools is considered China's golden age of philosophy, but was supressed by the Qin dynasty through burning of books and buring of scholars.

**mandate of heaven**

**Emperor Qin**

Emperor Qin was the emperor of the Qin dynasty which followed wars of conquest in the Warring States Period. Qin was the first emperor of China as he was able to reuinfy all the warring states. The Confucian Ritual State lecture showed that while the hundred schools of thought emerged as China's golden age of philosophy, the Qin dynastic rejected this scholasticisim and subsequently burned many of the texts and buried scholars. Opting for legalism instead, the Qin dynasty used social engineering and harsh punishment to force order and become efficient in military power. After conquering the warring states, they unified roads, measures, and occupations, whiling persecuting rujia scholars. The Qin dynasty lasted fourteen years and led to the Han Dynasty which followed legalist practices with the introduction of wuwei, as well as the recollection of the lost Confucian texts.

## Confucianism

**Life of Confucius**

Confucius was an ancient Chinese philosopher who lived in the Spring and Autumn period and was originally from the state of Lu. Examples of him come from the film *Confucius* (2010) that show his life beginning with his contravercial rescue of an ensalved boy sentenced to be buried with his dead master. While many of the teachings of the Confucian traditions are attributed to Confucius, it's important to know that these meditations were not invented by him; rather, they existed in China for centuries until he studied and gathered them together. His disciples then went on to write these collections down into the *Analects* and other various texts. Confucius's teachings on the concept of a junzi or ethical gentleman are significant because he poses them as accessible to anyone in any situation, age, or class (although it mainly applied to men). He lays out a path of self-cultivation that is open for anyway to take, which is part of why Confucian values have survived in China to today, even through the Maoist movements.

The *Analects* are part of the core texts of Confucianism. They were written by Confucius's disciples and follow all of his teachings. **the Dao**

The Dao, or the Way, is viewed as a natural harmony behind all of nature and everything, but also presents one of the first of many paradoxes of Daoism. According to the *Daodejing*, the Dao is self-existent, formless, and eternal. It is beyond understanding. In the Philosophical Taoism textbook chapter and discussion panel section, one of the first ideas of the Dao is that the Dao that we speak of is not the real Dao. This is an immediate impass is our understanding of the Dao, and simply forces us to just give up. The idea of the Dao blends together with Zen Buddhism in their thoughts of not doing anything and just following nature. The scriptures describe ideal governments and the ideal sages as just living by the Dao in a harmonious way. If you try to be Zen or try to understand the Dao, you've already gone down the wrong path. The Dao contrasts with other traditions in its way of not establishing clear guideline or morals, but just doing whatever. The Dao is the ultimate reality behind all phenomena.

**yin and yang**

Yin and yang is a symbol in daoism that shows duality. The Philosophical Taoism textbook chapter shows that in many other traditions, the categories of light and dark, good, and evil, true and wrong, and other opposites are brought up with one side being objectively good or sacred and the other being objectively bad or profane. The yin and yang, however, shows a harmonious outlook on nature depicting that all dualities exist together within each other and cannot just be by themselves.  Yin represents stillness and reception, and yang represents activity and movement. One cannot exist without the other.

**wuwei**

Wuwei translates non-action, and is one of the core ideas of philosophical Daoism. However, the Philosophical Taoism textbook chapter shows that flowing or going with the flow is a better description of wuwei. Wuwei is a non-purposeful, non-manipulative action based on naturalness and spontaneity without interference or artificial action. This idea of unforced living corresponds to the characteristics of Japanese Zen Buddhism in it's approaches to not thinking for spontaneous enlightenment. Both beliefs surrender to the idea of just just being without thinking about it too much. Laozi describes it like water, that thought water is soft and yielding, it can wear down the hardest rock. He advocates for being like water, to be yielding, receptive, yet powerful. Kings and warriors also took on this idea, like the Japanese samarai with Zen Buddhism, where an aggressors action is used against him. They believe that with non-action, anything can be done, and this belief has also been applied to governing as well.

**qi**

Qi is the substance that all the self is composed of, including the mental, physical, and spiritual parts. It can mean vapor or air, and in the Personal Dimensions textbook chapter it is the matter and energy that underlies all beings. In Daoist cosmology, qi at the time of creation became yin energy and the yang energy, which is what are also expressed in people. Qi is important since the Chinese have recognize the interconnections of the mind and body for centuries, and they described qi with a blanced flow of health, vitality, and spiritual power. Some sects try to balance and cultivate qi, and describe it as a vital force requiring its flow to be unimpeded.
